Engines of Creation

By: Josh Patterson
October 2026

William Nordhaus traced the cost of computation from calculation by hand to modern computers. Depending on the measure, he estimated that computing performance improved by a factor of between 1.7 trillion and 76 trillion relative to manual computing.[1]

Cheap computation created work that did not exist before. The Census Bureau added "artificial intelligence specialist" to its list of occupations in 2000 and "cybersecurity analyst" in 2018.[2] Lighting and cloth followed the same path over two centuries, as "Demand" showed, and each fall in cost opened uses that nobody had planned.

Automation Lowers Costs Continuously

In 1936, the aeronautical engineer T. P. Wright found that the labor cost of building an airplane fell by a fixed share, about 20 percent, each time the number of planes built doubled.[3] Béla Nagy and colleagues tested this rule against historical data on the cost and production of 62 technologies and found that it produced the best forecasts of the six rules they compared.[4]

Under Wright's law, cost keeps falling with every doubling of output and never reaches zero. The price of LLM output has been falling quickly too, as "Demand" showed, and use has grown faster than the price has fallen.

Cheap outputs become inputs to new products. Martin Weitzman modeled growth as new ideas formed by combining existing ones, so the number of possible combinations grows faster than the stock of ideas and growth is limited by the capacity to work through them.[5] W. Brian Arthur made the same point about technologies, which are assembled from earlier technologies.[6]

The title of this article borrows from Eric Drexler's 1986 book on nanotechnology, which imagined machines that could build almost anything at very low cost.[7] Drexler's machines are an extreme version of the cost declines that ordinary automation produces.

Why Work Is Inexhaustible

The belief that there is a fixed amount of work to go around has a name, the "lump of labour" fallacy, and economists have criticized it since the 1890s.[8]

Fears that machines would leave most people without work have recurred for two centuries. So far they have been wrong about the long run, though the adjustment was slow and costly for the workers caught in it.[9]

Part 5 has given three reasons why the demand for work keeps growing:

  • human wants are relative and rise as they are met ("The Evolution of Work")
  • each fall in cost opens new categories of goods, which need new kinds of work ("Demand")
  • each cheap output becomes a component of new outputs

If Machines Can Do Every Task

Wassily Leontief compared human workers to farm horses, and "Demand" explained why the comparison fails: people buy what machines produce, and horses do not. The harder question is what happens if machines can do every task that people can.

Anton Korinek and Donghyun Suh model this case. If the complexity of the tasks people can perform has a limit and machines reach it, wages first rise as the economy grows more productive and then collapse. If tasks keep getting more complex without limit, and enough of them stay beyond the machines' reach, wages can keep rising.[10]

William Nordhaus proposed several tests in economic data for signs that such a transition had begun. Most came out negative, and he concluded that it is not near.[11]

Two things would still limit what machines replace. People value some work because a person does it, as "The Evolution of Work" showed with handmade goods and with writing labeled as human. Korinek and Suh show that if a society keeps enough jobs human by choice, e.g., priests and judges, labor can stay scarce enough for wages to keep growing even after full automation becomes possible.[10] A job also carries responsibility for the result, and "What Is Work?" explained why an LLM agent cannot hold one.

The rest depends on ownership. David Autor, whose "paradox of abundance" "The Value of Human Labor" described, pointed out that horses do not own capital and people do. If machines make human labor much less scarce, he wrote, "the challenge will not be finding jobs for people to do, but rather finding a means to distribute our abundant societal riches."[12]

Tools Have Shaped Our Ancestors for Three Million Years

The oldest known stone tools, from Lomekwi in Kenya, are about 3.3 million years old, older than any known fossil of our genus.[13] As "The Neolithic Age" described, our hominin ancestors made tools before there were humans in the modern sense.

Tools changed human bodies. Human hands have features that make the forceful precision grips of toolmaking easier.[14] Making stone tools draws on brain regions that overlap with some of those used for language.[15] Richard Wrangham argues that cooking let our ancestors get more energy from food, which allowed smaller guts and larger brains.[16]

People also depend on inherited know-how to survive. Joseph Henrich describes the Burke and Wills expedition of 1860–61 across Australia. Robert Burke and William Wills died on the return journey in country where the local Aboriginal people lived well.

They had eaten nardoo seed without processing it the way local people did, and in Henrich's account they managed to "starve and poison themselves while keeping their stomachs full." The one survivor of their party, John King, lived because local people took him in and fed him.[17]

Stone flakes are tools. Machines and automation came much later:

Tool

A tool extends a capability, as a stone flake does.

Machine

A machine transmits or transforms force, as a lever or a loom does.

Automation

Automation adds control over a sequence of steps, so a machine can perform steps that a person used to perform. The punched cards of the Jacquard loom did this for woven patterns in the early 1800s,[18] and software does it today.

Tools as Engines of Creation

An engine converts energy into motion. An engine of creation, in the sense used here, converts human wants into goods and services at falling cost.

The cycle that "What Is Work?" laid out for Part 5 is a loop. New work from each turn becomes the next set of tasks and steps, and Part 4's civilization information layer keeps the improvements from one generation to the next.

Adam Smith saw knowledge work come out of this process. As the division of labor deepened, making machines became "the business of a peculiar trade," and some improvements came from "those who are called philosophers or men of speculation," whose trade was observation and who "are often capable of combining together the powers of the most distant and dissimilar objects."[19]

An engine also needs a purpose. Value is defined by human wants, so a machine running with nobody to want its output produces nothing of value. Part 7 takes up the philosophical version of this question, whether a tool can exist without people.

The Newest Engine

In the vocabulary of Part 5, AI is automation applied to the cognitive steps of knowledge work. "Labor Automation" defined automation as capital performing a step that labor used to perform. An LLM fits the definition. A firm buys or rents it, and it performs steps that change information, the kind of steps that "Physical Labor and Cognitive Labor" called cognitive.

Whether AI exists depends on what the word is asked to name. As a label, "AI" has followed the frontier of what machines could not yet do, and a technique that works soon gets a plainer name, as "AI Is Just Productivity You Don't Understand Yet" argued. As an economic object, AI shows up as a fall in the cost of a step.

In 2019, Ajay Agrawal, Joshua Gans, and Avi Goldfarb described machine learning as an advance in one part of intelligence, prediction, whose quality-adjusted cost was falling. Prediction is an input to decisions, which also need data, the ability to act, and judgment about what different outcomes are worth. As prediction gets better and cheaper, they wrote, "the demand for these related, complementary tasks may increase." They found forces pushing the demand for labor both up and down, and could not say which would win.[20]

An LLM predicts text, so the steps it makes cheaper are the steps of knowledge work that produce text. Its effects on labor run through the same channels as every earlier engine, and most claims that AI will take all the jobs leave out at least one of them:

Common claimWhat Part 5 found
AI will take over whole jobs.A job is a bundle of tasks, and each task is a sequence of steps. AI takes over steps, and most jobs keep steps it does not perform. ("The Task Structure of Work")
AI agents will become virtual employees.An agent can perform work, but it cannot sell its labor, enter an employment contract, or carry liability, so a person still holds the job. ("What Is Work?")
Machines that think will replace people who think.The cognitive steps most likely to stay human are those whose outputs someone must verify or answer for. ("Physical Labor and Cognitive Labor")
Automation makes human labor worthless.Labor is valued by its scarcity, and automation moves the scarcity to the steps that remain. ("The Value of Human Labor")
Work done by machines disappears.The work continues in the machine, and checking its output becomes new work for people. ("Labor Automation")
Cheaper output means fewer workers.Where buyers want much more at a lower price, cheaper output brings more work, and freed income goes to new goods that need new work. ("Demand")
People will eventually have enough.Wants are relative and rise with everyone's living standard. ("The Evolution of Work")

So far the labor-market effects of LLMs have been small, as "Labor Automation" found, and the clearest signal is slower hiring for entry-level jobs in the most exposed occupations.

Where Work Goes From Here

The mechanisms of Part 5 point to several trends for the coming years:

  • The price of cognitive steps will keep falling, and firms will use far more machine-generated text and analysis, as they did with computing.
  • Inside most jobs, the main near-term effect will be augmentation. People keep the job and hand some of its steps to the tool, as "Labor Automation" found for LLMs so far, and checking the tool's output becomes part of the work.
  • Jobs will be rebuilt around the steps that machines do poorly or that people want a person to do, e.g., deciding what to do, taking responsibility for the result, and dealing with people.
  • LLMs will narrow skill differences on the steps they touch. In the experiments described in "The Value of Human Labor," the least experienced workers gained the most. That lowers the premium for those skills and lets more people do the work, the trade-off David Autor and Neil Thompson traced in clerical jobs.
  • Entry-level jobs are the most exposed, and they are where people build expertise. In robotic surgery, Matthew Beane found that the technology cut trainees' part in operations so much that approved ways of learning stopped working. Only a minority became competent, by learning in ways that challenged their programs' norms and policies.[21]

New work will keep appearing, and where it appears has changed. From 1940 to 1980, much new work was in middle-paid production and clerical occupations. Since 1980, it has appeared mainly in high-paid professional occupations and secondarily in low-paid services.[22]

Autor sorts recent new occupations into three groups:[23]

  • "frontier jobs" that produce, install, and deploy new technologies, which are relatively well paid and held disproportionately by college-educated men
  • "wealth work," in-person services for affluent consumers such as personal training and pet care, which pays close to the local average and is held disproportionately by women
  • "last mile" jobs that carry out the human parts of nearly automated tasks, such as call-center and order-fulfillment work, where wages and education levels are well below average

He concluded that "the bifurcated structure of new work does not suggest that a technology-driven 'reinstatement' of middle-skill, non-college jobs is currently underway."[23]

Autor has also argued that AI, used well, could let more workers with foundational training make some of the higher-stakes decisions now reserved for elite experts, much as nurse practitioners came to diagnose patients and prescribe medications, work once reserved for physicians. He presents this as a possible path that depends on how AI is used.[24]

For a business, the planning question is which products and services become worth offering when cognitive steps get cheap, along with which costs come down.

New Work That Reaches Most People

Daron Acemoglu, Autor, and Simon Johnson sort technologies by what they do to workers' expertise, which has value in the labor market only when it is required for some valuable activity and scarce. Automation commodifies expertise and makes it less valuable. Tools that let less expert workers do tasks that used to need experts open opportunities for some workers and increase competition for others. Technology that creates new tasks is the one category they find unambiguously good for workers, because it creates demand for new expertise.[25]

The research behind Part 5 points to several conditions for new work that reaches most people:

  • More AI development needs to aim at creating new tasks for people. Firms and AI developers have reasons to automate beyond what productivity alone justifies, as "Labor Automation" described, and governments can shift the balance through what they buy and how they pay for services in fields such as health care and education.[25]
  • US tax rules favor machines. Acemoglu, Andrea Manera, and Pascual Restrepo estimate effective tax rates of 25.5 to 33.5 percent on labor, against about 5 percent on equipment and software after the 2017 tax reform, and argue that the difference has pushed automation beyond what is socially desirable.[26]
  • Access to the tools, and to the skills to use them, is uneven. In a survey of Danish workers in 11 occupations exposed to ChatGPT, women were 16 percentage points less likely than men to have used it for work, and lower earners were less likely to use it.[27] Adoption overall has been fast, as "The Evolution of Work" showed.
  • Entry-level jobs have to stay places where people learn. Firms that hand junior steps to machines need apprenticeships or supervised practice in their place.
  • The people and places hit first need help. After the China trade shock, job losses in the most exposed US regions lasted into 2019, nearly a decade after the shock peaked, and the main way people adjusted was by leaving work. Losses were larger in places that started with fewer college-educated workers.[28] New work, as "What Is Work?" showed, has formed mostly in cities with more college graduates.
  • Workers need a say in how the tools are used and a share in what they produce. Ways for workers to have input into how AI is developed and deployed are among the policies Acemoglu, Autor, and Johnson discuss,[25] and the ownership of machines decides who receives the income they earn, the question raised in "The Value of Human Labor" and "The Evolution of Work."

Which cognitive steps an LLM can perform reliably is still an open question, and so is whether producing fluent text about a problem amounts to reasoning about it. Part 6 starts with the prior question, "What Is Reasoning?"
