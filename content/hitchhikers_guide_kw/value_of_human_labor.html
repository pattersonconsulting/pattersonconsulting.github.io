The Value of Human Labor

By: Josh Patterson
October 2026

Computers automated routine work for two groups of clerks: accounting clerks, and stock and inventory clerks. Standard task models predict similar effects on both. Between 1980 and 2018, the outcomes went in opposite directions.

Accounting clerks fell from 1.62 million to 1.11 million while their average wage rose about 39 percent. Inventory clerks nearly tripled in number, from about 540,000 to 1.48 million, and their average wage fell about 13 percent.[1]

Accounting clerks, 1980–2018

Employment 1.62 million → 1.11 million. Average wage up about 39 percent. Automation took the least expert work.

Inventory clerks, 1980–2018

Employment about 540,000 → 1.48 million. Average wage down about 13 percent. Automation took the most expert work.

David Autor and Neil Thompson explain the difference by which steps were automated. For accounting clerks, the automated work was basic bookkeeping, the least expert part of the job. What remained, such as reconciliation and problem-solving, was more expert, so fewer workers qualified and pay rose.

Inventory clerks lost the expert parts of their job, such as computing prices, and what remained was generic. More workers could do it, and pay fell.

Three Measures of Value

Discussions of "the value of labor" often merge three numbers:

Wage

The price of an hour of labor.

Value added

The value of what the work produces, minus purchased inputs.

Labor share

Wages divided by value added: the part of the work's value paid to workers.

In Robert Solow's US data for 1909–1949, labor received roughly 60 to 67 percent of income.[2]

Adam Smith noticed that usefulness and price can diverge: "Nothing is more useful than water; but it will purchase scarce any thing," while a diamond "has scarce any value in use; but a very great quantity of other goods may frequently be had in exchange for it."[3]

Later economists resolved the puzzle by looking at the margin, where price depends on scarcity. Labor works the same way. The wage for a step reflects how scarce the ability to do it is, more than how important the step is to the final product.

How Economists Have Valued Labor

Each major model ties the wage to something different:

ModelWhat sets the wage
Marginal productivityIn a competitive market, the wage equals the value of what the last hour of labor produces.[4]
Human capitalSkills are an investment, and earnings rise with schooling and experience.[5]
Compensating differencesWages adjust for hard, dirty, or risky work, a point Smith made in 1776.[3]
Comparative advantageWorkers sort into the jobs where their particular skills pay best, so the same skill earns different amounts in different jobs.[6]
O-ring productionWhen quality multiplies across steps, workers of similar skill cluster together, and small differences in skill produce large differences in output and pay.[7]
SuperstarsWhen one person's work can reach a large market, small differences in talent produce large differences in earnings.[8]
Scarce expertiseThe wage reflects how few workers can perform the most expert task left in the job.[1]

Real labor markets also depart from competition. Employers with wage-setting power pay less than workers' marginal product.[9] When steps are complements, the contribution of any one worker is hard to isolate, and wages are better described as the outcome of bargaining over joint output.[10]

Value at the Step Level

The same distinctions apply inside a task. For each step, it helps to separate the hours of labor, their cost, the units of output, the value of that output, the hours a machine now performs, and the net value of that automation after its cost and any loss of quality.

These numbers diverge within a single task. In the claims workflow from "The Task Structure of Work," most of the hours go to gathering and documenting evidence, while most of the value and the risk sit in the coverage decision and the payout estimate.

The colors match the automation decisions in "Labor Automation." In this example, the three steps marked for automation take 60 percent of the hours and carry 20 percent of the value. The coverage check and the payout estimate take 25 percent of the hours and carry 60 percent of the value.

Automating the steps with the most hours can save a great deal of labor while barely changing the value of the work. An error in the decision step can erase the value of every other step. Part 10 builds these differences into a value model for knowledge-work roles.

What Automation Does to the Value of Labor

Automation changes the value of labor in several ways at once:

  • A ceiling on automated steps. Once capital can perform a step more cheaply, a firm will not pay more for human time in that step than the machine costs. Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo call the resulting loss of tasks for labor the displacement effect.[11]
  • More value in the remaining steps. In an O-ring chain, more reliable automated steps raise the value of the human steps that remain.[12] Joshua Gans and Avi Goldfarb show that labor income can rise under partial automation.[10]
  • A moving expertise threshold. Automating inexpert steps tends to raise wages and lower employment, and automating expert steps does the reverse, as with the two clerks.[1]
  • New work with a fading premium. In 2011–2023, workers in job titles introduced since 1970 earned about 1.8 percent more than similar workers in older titles, and about 3.8 percent more in technology-linked titles. The premium was largest for titles introduced after 2000 and had disappeared for titles from the 1970s.[13]

The first studies of generative AI at work point the same way. At a large customer-support operation, an AI assistant raised issues resolved per hour by about 15 percent on average and by about 30 percent for less-skilled and less-experienced agents. The most skilled agents saw small gains in speed and small declines in quality.[14]

In an experiment with professional writing tasks, ChatGPT cut the time taken by about 40 percent and raised quality by about 18 percent, with the largest gains for weaker writers.[15] In both cases the tool narrowed skill differences on the steps it touched, which lowers the premium for those skills and raises average output.

Who Gets the Gains

For most people, the main income-generating asset is their labor, and it pays because it is scarce. Autor calls the resulting risk the "paradox of abundance": society as a whole gets richer, while people who own labor and no capital can struggle to earn a living.[16] Across 19 developed economies from 1970 to 2007, productivity growth raised employment overall and lowered labor's share of value added.[17]

The value of a human step depends on how scarce the ability to do it is, compared with the cheapest alternative, including a machine. For a business case, that means estimating the value of the remaining steps, and who will be able to do them, as well as the hours saved in the automated ones.
