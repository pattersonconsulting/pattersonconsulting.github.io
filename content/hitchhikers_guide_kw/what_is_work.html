What Is Work?

By: Josh Patterson
September 2026

Every organization runs on work.

Every technology investment is ultimately a bet on how work will change: which tasks can be automated, which become more valuable, and which disappear. The debate over large language models and employment is no different. Yet the central concept in that debate—work—is rarely defined precisely.

"Work," "labor," and "job" are often used interchangeably, but they describe different things.

The distinction has practical consequences. Frey and Osborne (2013) assessed entire occupations and estimated that 47 percent of US employment was in occupations at high risk of automation, defined as an estimated probability above 70 percent.[1] Arntz, Gregory, and Zierahn (2016) revisited that estimate by examining the tasks individual workers actually reported performing. The estimated share of US employment at high risk fell to 9 percent, matching the average across 21 OECD countries.[2]

These concepts are related, but they are not interchangeable. Distinguishing them is essential to understanding how technology changes work and what that means for employment.

Labor, Work, and Jobs

Hannah Arendt (1958) drew one of the most influential distinctions between labor and work.[3] Labor is the activity tied to the body's biological life. Its products are consumed almost as fast as they are made, and it leaves nothing behind. Arendt's own example is bread, which lasts about a day. Work fabricates durable things that together make up a human world. Her matching example is a table, which can outlast generations.

Writing something down also counts as work for Arendt, because it turns passing speech and thought into a lasting record.

Marx (1867), writing almost a century before Arendt, drew a different line, between labor and labor-power.[4] Labor is the process by which people act on nature to turn raw material into useful things. What workers sell in the market is labor-power: their capacity to work, sold for a set period. Labor-power is the commodity that gets priced, hired, and tracked on a payroll. Arendt later criticized Marx for treating all labor as if it were work.

Neither of these definitions is a job. A job is an institutional construct: a specific bundle of tasks packaged into an employment contract at a particular point in time. Jobs are created and destroyed continuously as technology and economic context change. In its 1966 report, the National Commission on Technology, Automation, and Economic Progress, a blue-ribbon panel appointed by President Johnson, put it this way. Autor (2015b) quotes the passage:

"The basic fact is that technology eliminates jobs, not work."

— National Commission on Technology, Automation, and Economic Progress (1966), Technology and the American Economy, Vol. I, p. 9; quoted in Autor (2015b), p. 4[5]

Labor

The human capacity for effort, treated as an economic resource. Labor is expended over time, must be replenished, and is priced in units of time. Workers sell their labor-power: their capacity to perform work for a defined period.

Work

Purposeful human effort directed toward producing something of value within a given social and economic context. As societies become more complex, the demand for work expands and changes.

Job

A defined bundle of tasks organized around an employment relationship at a particular point in time. Jobs are created, reshaped, and eliminated as technology and economic conditions change.

By around 3,000 BCE, the temple households of Uruk employed trained record-keepers, usually called scribes. They maintained clay-tablet accounts of grain, livestock, land, and labor, learning their trade through standardized exercises in signs and word lists.[6] This was a job in the sense defined here: a recognizable bundle of tasks organized around a specific role.

The surviving evidence does not establish how these early record-keepers were compensated. A thousand years later, under the Third Dynasty of Ur, ordinary scribes received rations of barley, oil, and cloth, and some were allocated fields.[6]

The scribal job is gone. People still record transactions, keep institutional records, and coordinate economic activity at scale.

Over the centuries those tasks have been regrouped into other jobs, such as the clerk, the bookkeeper, and the accountant, usually as record-keeping tools changed.

The Emergent Nature of Work

What counts as work, and what that work is worth, depends on the social and economic context, and that context keeps changing.

Before agriculture, people lived in small mobile bands. Studies of recent foragers find that getting food took a few hours a day; Sahlins (1972) put it at three to five.[7] Counting food processing, tool-making, and camp chores raises the total to around 40 hours a week.[8]

In either case, there was no demand for scribes, administrators, architects, or lawyers because the institutions that required their services did not yet exist. There were no written records to maintain, large organizations to administer, or permanent buildings to design. Agriculture generated surplus, surplus supported settled communities, and those communities developed systems for keeping records.

Small clay tokens, each standing for a measure of grain or a head of livestock, appear in Near Eastern villages around the time farming began.[9] By the late fourth millennium BCE, the temple households of Uruk were using written tablets to track grain stores, field areas, and the rations owed to work gangs.[6] The demand for accounting, record-keeping, and institutional coordination came with agriculture and the larger, more complex organizations it made possible. Part 4 traced this sequence in detail.

Work grows as societies become more complex, because complexity creates needs that did not exist before, such as the record-keeping Uruk's temple households required.

Modern data show something similar. Autor, Chin, Salomons, and Seegmiller (2024) estimate that roughly 60 percent of US employment in 2018 was in job titles the Census Bureau added after 1940, and they trace this new work to new technologies and to shifts in demand.[10]

Automation also creates a tension that Autor (2015a) calls a "paradox of abundance."[11]

For most people, the main income-generating asset is their labor, and it pays because it is scarce. If technology substituted cheap capital for that labor, society as a whole would be richer, but people who own labor and no capital could struggle to earn a living. Autor treats this as a distribution problem: total output rises, and the question is who receives it.

New work, meaning job titles that did not exist before, offsets some of what automation takes away (Autor, Chin, Salomons, and Seegmiller 2026). It tends to appear where demand for new products and services is growing, and it requires expertise that is scarce at first. Workers in new job titles earn a wage premium, and that premium is smaller for older vintages of new work as the expertise spreads.[12]

Mokyr, Vickers, and Ziebarth (2015) traced fears of technological unemployment from the Industrial Revolution through the Great Depression. In each period, predictions of lasting mass unemployment did not come true. Machines raised demand for complementary work, and new products created new sectors. Gains for workers came slowly and unevenly. The authors expect the pattern to broadly hold for AI and robotics, but warn that the transition may be painful and that some workers may need income support.[13]

The Task Structure of Work

Autor, Levy, and Murnane (2003) built their analysis around tasks.[14] They sorted tasks by whether a computer could carry them out by following explicit rules (routine) or not (nonroutine), and by whether the task was analytic, interactive, or manual. Later work grouped these into three categories: abstract, routine, and manual.[15] In that framework a task is "a unit of work activity that produces output," and a job is a bundle of tasks.

Abstract Tasks

Problem-solving, intuition, persuasion, and creativity. Computers complement these tasks.

Routine Tasks

Tasks that follow explicit rules, cognitive or manual, e.g. bookkeeping, sorting, or repetitive assembly. Computers substitute for them.

Manual Tasks

Nonroutine manual tasks that need situational adaptability, e.g. driving in city traffic or preparing a meal. Computers neither substitute for nor complement these much, unless the environment can be controlled.

Autor and Handel (2013) found that tasks vary within occupations as well as between them. Workers with the same job title report doing different tasks, and those differences predict wages even after controlling for occupation.[16] When asking how technology will affect a worker, their tasks tell you more than their job title.

When Human Activity Stops Being Work

Human activity ceases to be economically viable as work when demand for the human effort behind it collapses.

The activity itself may continue, but the market no longer needs people to perform it.

The handloom weavers were right about what the power loom would do to them. Their pay was already unsteady during the Napoleonic wars, when too many weavers competed for too little work. After 1826, as power looms spread, piece-rates collapsed and never recovered. In north-east Lancashire, the calico piece-rate in 1841 was about a sixth of its 1814 level.[17][18]

As cloth got cheaper, people bought far more of it. In the United States, labor productivity in cotton cloth rose about 30-fold over the nineteenth century while consumption rose about 100-fold, and employment in the industry grew.[19]

Demand for hand-weaving collapsed. Power looms, run for long factory hours by lower-paid workers and made steadily faster, produced cloth more cheaply, and by the 1840s hand-weaving of cotton had largely disappeared in Britain.[18]

Telephone operators followed a related path. When AT&T switched cities to dial service between 1920 and 1940, young women's employment as operators fell 50 to 80 percent.[20] Women already working as operators were more likely to end up in lower-paying jobs or out of work. Later cohorts found work as typists, secretaries, and in service jobs, some of them new kinds of work, and overall employment for young women did not fall.

Two parts of the weaver story did not repeat. Local wages held up, and AT&T raised phone rates after automation instead of passing on its savings.

In each of these cases, automation removed specific jobs, and new kinds of work appeared elsewhere.

Smith (1776) described an early form of this.[21] He argued that dividing labor makes workers far more productive, partly through machines that let one worker do the work of many. He also noted that the division of labor produced new trades, such as machine-making and "philosophers, or men of speculation," and that a larger market supports finer specialization than a small one.

The Evolution of Work

Work is purposeful human effort directed toward producing value within a given social and economic context.

Labor is the economic input: the capacity for that effort, priced and hired in the market.

A job is a specific bundle of tasks organized into a role at a particular point in time.

Technology eliminates some jobs by automating their tasks, while changing the economy in ways that create demand for new work and new roles. Historically, new work has eventually offset the jobs automation displaced, but the transition has been slow and uneven, and the workers affected have not shared equally in the gains.

The "civilization information layer" traced in Part 4 made abstract task work possible at scale. In the United States, professional, technical, and managerial occupations—jobs built largely around abstract tasks—grew by about 3 percentage points of total employment per decade from 1940 to 1980, with faster growth from 1980 to 2010, though that growth slowed after 2000.[22]

The question now is whether AI can automate the judgment these jobs require, or whether its capabilities remain concentrated in retrieving information and generating outputs. That distinction will shape how AI changes knowledge work and the jobs built around it.
